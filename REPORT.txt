Project 10 — Attacker and Defender  
Normal Tier Reflection  
Haya Hussein

1. Overview
This project required building both sides of a security incident: the attacker’s kill chain and the defender’s detection pipeline.
I created a realistic phishing lure, simulated credential theft, lateral movement, privilege escalation, donor‑table harvesting, and data exfiltration. 
On the defender side, I implemented deterministic detection rules, ran triage with an AI assistant, and compared human‑written rules to AI analysis.

2. What I Built (Attacker Side)
I wrote a phishing email targeting the ministry’s finance volunteer. The attacker used stolen credentials to log in from Romania, which appeared in the auth.log as a non‑home‑geo login. 
The attacker escalated privileges on app1 using sudo without a change ticket, then moved laterally to db1. 
Once inside db1, the attacker performed multiple SELECT queries against the donor table in a short time window, indicating data harvesting.
 Finally, the attacker exfiltrated donor data using a Markdown image link pointing to an external IP.

3. What I Built (Defender Side)
I used the baseline triage_logins.py rules and added two new rules:
- Impossible travel (same user logging in from different countries within 30 minutes)
- Bulk donor-table read (multiple SELECTs within 10 seconds)

My detect.py successfully flagged:
- Burst of denied auths
- Non‑home‑geo logins
- Sudo without a change ticket
- Exfiltration to attacker IP
- Impossible travel (RO → INT)
- Bulk donor-table read

These rules provided reliable, deterministic detection of the attacker’s behavior.

4. AI Triage Summary
I used a local AI model to summarize the auth.log. The AI correctly identified suspicious logins, sudo activity, and db1 access. It provided a readable narrative of the intrusion.

However, the AI missed:
- Impossible travel
- Bulk SELECT queries
- The full kill chain sequence
- The exfiltration pattern

The AI also added small assumptions not present in the logs. This showed that while AI is helpful for summarizing, it is not reliable for detection.

5. Comparison: AI vs Deterministic Rules
The deterministic rules were precise and caught every major indicator. The AI was helpful for explanation but not dependable for detection. 
The AI did not identify several critical steps of the attack, while the rules caught them immediately.

My conclusion: AI can support analysts, but it cannot replace rule‑based detection in SOC environments.

6. Honest AI Usage Note
I used an AI assistant lightly during this project:
- To brainstorm clearer wording for the phishing email
- To explain the concept of “impossible travel”
- To outline what typically goes into a scenario description
- To tidy the wording in one section of my report

I wrote all final content myself, including the code, rules, scenario, triage, threat map, and this report.

7. Final Reflection
This project helped me understand how attackers chain multiple steps together and how defenders must rely on deterministic rules to detect them. 
It also showed the limits of AI in security operations. AI is useful for summarizing and brainstorming, but reliable detection requires explicit logic, careful rule design, and human judgment.
